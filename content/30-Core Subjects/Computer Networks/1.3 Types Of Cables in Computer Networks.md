### Types of Cables in Computer Networks

In computer networks, cables are the primary medium through which data is transmitted between devices. Each cable type has unique characteristics that make it suitable for different scenarios.

---

### **1. Unshielded Twisted Pair (UTP) Cable**

UTP cables are made of pairs of twisted wires, which help reduce electromagnetic interference. UTP is widely used in Ethernet networks.

- **10 Base T**:
  - **Speed**: 10 Mbps (megabits per second).
  - **Base**: Refers to **Baseband**, which means the cable is carrying a single data signal at a time, unlike broadband which can carry multiple signals.
  - **T**: Stands for **Twisted Pair**, indicating the physical structure of the cable.
  - **Range**: Up to **100 meters**.

- **100 Base T** (Also known as Fast Ethernet):
  - **Speed**: 100 Mbps.
  - **Base**: Still uses **Baseband** transmission.
  - **T**: Twisted Pair.
  - **Range**: Up to **100 meters**.

**Advantages**:

- UTP cables are affordable and easy to install.
- They are commonly used in LANs (Local Area Networks).

---

### **2. Coaxial Cable**

Coaxial cables consist of a central conductor, an insulating layer, a metallic shield, and an outer insulating layer. Coaxial cables offer better shielding against interference and are used in older Ethernet networks.

- **10 Base 2** (Also called **Thinnet**):
  - **Speed**: 10 Mbps.
  - **Base**: Baseband.
  - **2**: Refers to the maximum length of **200 meters** between network nodes.

- **10 Base 5** (Also called **Thicknet**):
  - **Speed**: 10 Mbps.
  - **Base**: Baseband.
  - **5**: Refers to the maximum length of **500 meters** between network nodes.

**Advantages**:

- Coaxial cables provide good shielding against electromagnetic interference.
- They were used in early Ethernet networks but have largely been replaced by UTP and fiber optic cables.

---

### **3. Fiber Optic Cable**

Fiber optic cables transmit data as light signals rather than electrical signals, providing high speed and long-distance transmission. They consist of a core (usually made of glass) and cladding that reflects light back into the core.

- **100 Base Fx**:
  - **Speed**: 100 Mbps.
  - **Base**: Baseband.
  - **Fx**: Refers to **Fiber Optic** cable.
  - **Range**: Can reach up to **2 kilometers** (multi-mode fiber) or more for single-mode fiber.

**Advantages**:

- Fiber optic cables are immune to electromagnetic interference, which makes them ideal for environments with a lot of electronic noise.
- They support higher speeds and longer transmission distances compared to UTP and coaxial cables.

---

### **Explanation of Terms**:

- **Base (Baseband)**: Refers to the use of the entire bandwidth of the cable for a single data signal. **Baseband** signals do not share the medium with other signals. In contrast, **broadband** communication (used in technologies like cable TV) allows multiple signals over a single cable by using different frequencies.

- **10, 100 (Speed)**: These numbers represent the speed of the data transmission over the cable. For example, "10 Base" stands for 10 Mbps, and "100 Base" refers to 100 Mbps.

- **T (Twisted Pair)**: Indicates that the cable is a twisted-pair type, which is common in Ethernet networks. Twisting the wires helps reduce interference from external sources.

- **2, 5 (Range)**: These numbers, as seen in **10 Base 2** and **10 Base 5**, represent the maximum distance (in hundreds of meters) that the signal can travel without needing a repeater.

### Conclusion:

Different types of cables are used based on the requirements of the network, including speed, distance, and environmental factors. UTP cables are widely used for shorter distances in office networks, coaxial cables are used in legacy systems, and fiber optics are preferred for long-distance, high-speed connections. Understanding the various standards like **Base T** and **Base 5** helps in selecting the right cable for a specific networking scenario.
