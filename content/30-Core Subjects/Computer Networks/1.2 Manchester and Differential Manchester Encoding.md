### Manchester Encoding

Manchester encoding is a **bi-phase encoding scheme** used in data communication to ensure synchronization between the sender and receiver. It combines the clock and data into a single self-synchronizing data stream by using transitions (changes in voltage level) to represent bits.

---

### Manchester Encoding

Manchester encoding represents binary data using transitions (voltage changes) at the middle of each bit period, which helps with synchronization.

#### 1. **Dr. Thomas Convention** (GEC Standard)

- **High to Low transition** at the midpoint represents **binary 1**.
- **Low to High transition** at the midpoint represents **binary 0**.

This convention was used by **Dr. Thomas** and is sometimes referred to as the **British GEC convention**. It was mainly used in **telecommunications** and specific proprietary systems.

#### 2. **IEEE 802.3 Ethernet Standard (IEEE Convention)**

- **Low to High transition** at the midpoint represents **binary 1**.
- **High to Low transition** at the midpoint represents **binary 0**.

This is the convention followed by the **IEEE 802.3 Ethernet standard**, widely used in Ethernet networks.

---

### Differential Manchester Encoding

**Differential Manchester Encoding** is an enhancement of standard Manchester encoding, where the **presence or absence of a transition** at the start of a bit interval encodes the data, rather than the voltage level itself.

- **Transition at the start of the bit period**: This indicates a **binary 0**.
- **No transition at the start of the bit period**: This indicates a **binary 1**.
- There is **always a transition** at the midpoint of the bit period for clocking purposes, making it highly reliable for synchronization.

**Key Features**:

- **Resistant to polarity inversions**: Since the encoding is based on transitions rather than absolute voltage levels, it is less affected by signal distortions or polarity reversals.
- **More robust than Manchester encoding** in environments where noise or interference is present.

**Use Cases**:

- Differential Manchester encoding is often used in **magnetic and optical storage devices** and was also employed in protocols like **Token Ring networks**.
