### Hubs ([Physical Layer](1.%20Physical%20Layer.md))

**1. Multiport Repeater**

- A hub is essentially a multiport [repeater](1.4.1%20Repeaters.md), functioning as a central connection point for multiple devices in a local area network (LAN).
- It connects several Ethernet devices, allowing them to communicate within the same network segment by receiving and forwarding data packets.

**2. Forwarding**

- Hubs forward incoming data packets to all connected ports, regardless of the intended recipient.
- This means that when a device sends a packet to the hub, it broadcasts the packet to all other connected devices, which can lead to network congestion.

**3. No Filtering**

- Similar to repeaters, hubs do not filter or process data; they operate at the physical layer and lack any intelligence to determine where to send data packets.
- As a result, all data is forwarded without consideration of destination addresses, contributing to increased traffic and potential collisions.

**4. Collision Domain - 'n'**

- A hub creates a single collision domain that includes all devices connected to it, meaning that if two or more devices transmit simultaneously, a collision occurs.
- The number of devices (n) connected to the hub directly affects the likelihood of collisions, which can lead to data loss and reduced network efficiency as more devices share the same bandwidth.

### Summary

In summary, hubs are basic networking devices that connect multiple devices in a LAN by broadcasting data packets to all ports without filtering. However, their operation within a single collision domain makes them less efficient for larger networks, often leading to congestion and performance issues.
